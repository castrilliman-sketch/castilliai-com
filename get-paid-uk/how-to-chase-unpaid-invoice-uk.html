How to chase an unpaid invoice in the UK

A calm, written, step-by-step way to chase a late invoice — with wording you can copy, a worked example, and what the Late Payment Act lets you add on business-to-business invoices.

Quick answer: chase in writing, early and on a fixed schedule. Send a friendly reminder the day after the due date, a firmer one at 7 and 14 days, and a dated final request around day 30. On a late business-to-business invoice you can usually add statutory interest and a fixed sum of £40, £70 or £100. If nothing works, you can make a court claim for money.

Before you chase: check three things

  1. Is the invoice actually late? Use the payment date you agreed. If you never agreed one, the Late Payment Act treats a business-to-business payment as late 30 days after the customer received the invoice or you delivered the goods or service, whichever is later (section 4 of the 1998 Act; GOV.UK “When a payment becomes late”).
  2. Is your customer a business or a consumer? Statutory interest and the fixed sums only apply when you and your customer are both acting in the course of a business (public authorities count). They do not apply to private individuals buying for personal use.
  3. Who approves payment? Find the person or accounts inbox that actually pays invoices, and ask for the date of their next payment run. “We’re processing it” has no date; a payment run does.

Keep every chase on the same email thread, so there is one clear record of dates and replies. Pausing new work for a customer who is well overdue is your call, but say so in writing.

Wording you can adapt

Day 1 overdue

Hi [name], Invoice [number] for £[amount] was due on [date]. Could you confirm when it will be paid? I’ve attached a copy and my bank details are below. Thanks, [your name]

Day 14

Hi [name], Invoice [number] for £[amount] is now 14 days overdue. Please let me know today the date it will be paid. As this is a business-to-business invoice, I may add statutory interest and fixed compensation under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998 if it remains unpaid. Thanks, [your name]

Final request (around day 30)

Dear [name], Invoice [number] for £[amount], due on [date], remains unpaid despite reminders on [dates]. Please pay £[amount] plus statutory interest of £[interest] and fixed compensation of £[40/70/100] (total £[total]) by [deadline date]. If payment is not received by then, I intend to make a court claim for the amount owed without further notice. Yours sincerely, [your name]

Only say you will take a step if you are prepared to take it. Send a separate, new invoice for any interest and fixed sum rather than quietly changing the original — that is what GOV.UK suggests.

If they still don’t pay

In England and Wales you can apply to a county court to claim money you’re owed — what used to be called the small claims court. GOV.UK’s Make a court claim for money guide explains fees, mediation and enforcement. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own processes. Citizens Advice and Advicenow can help, or speak to a solicitor for advice on your case.

Common questions

When is an invoice legally late in the UK?

On the payment date you agreed. If no date was agreed, the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998 treats a business-to-business payment as late 30 days after the customer received the invoice or you delivered the goods or service, whichever is later.

How often should I chase an unpaid invoice?

A common schedule is a reminder the day after the due date, then at 7, 14 and around 30 days, finishing with a dated final request. Keep everything in writing and on one thread so you have a clear record.

Can I add interest when I chase a late invoice?

On a business-to-business invoice you can usually claim statutory interest at 8% above the Bank of England reference rate, plus a fixed sum of £40, £70 or £100, unless your contract sets a different late-payment rate. It does not apply to consumer customers.

What can I do if a client ignores every reminder?

Send a final request with a clear deadline. If it passes, you can make a court claim for money; GOV.UK explains the process for England and Wales, and Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own. Consider getting advice for larger or disputed debts.
